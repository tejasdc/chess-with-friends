# Two Chairs: Software that honors your intentions

*Working project thesis · September 10, 2026*

Two Chairs starts with chess between friends. Its broader ambition is to make software that embodies the way people want to spend their time: whom they want to be with, what they want to do together, and where they want the experience to end. Chess is the first concrete expression of that idea. Other games can carry it forward, and the underlying principle extends to software well beyond games.

The project began with a specific frustration. Tejas was trying to reduce social media use and found himself playing chess longer than he intended instead. An apparently better activity had inherited the same problem: there was always another opponent and another game. The desire was more particular than “play chess.” It was to play with friends, deliberately, at a time that worked for both people. The original requirements made that intention a product rule: a game happens when someone you know sits down across from you. [1, 2]

An available stranger can remove the wait for an opponent. Waiting for a friend makes the game depend on a relationship and another person's consent. In Two Chairs, that dependence is valuable. Friends have schedules; they have to agree to play; eventually one of them needs to leave. Those limits give the activity a place in the rest of life.

This is what “the constraint is the product” means here. Friends only, an agreed time, and a natural stopping point are part of the desired experience. Software can make those choices hold without asking the person to renegotiate them at every tempting moment. A constraint earns its place when it expresses an intention the person endorses. There is no general virtue in making a tool harder to use.

The current chess product makes that principle concrete. It excludes stranger matchmaking, ratings, ladders, streaks, and an endless puzzles feed. A rematch is welcome when the friend also wants one. Notifications serve an actual invitation, the completion of a request, or a time both people agreed to. A notification whose purpose is to manufacture another visit violates the project's stated policy. Reliability and ease still matter: accepting an invitation, reconnecting to a game, and talking to the other person should work smoothly. [1]

The name Two Chairs expresses the social unit. A table with a board and two seats gives people somewhere to spend time together. The project's visual reference, Rodchenko's chess table, makes that relationship physical: the board and the places for the players belong to one object. The optional voice call serves the same purpose inside the app. The game gives friends something to do while they are together. [1, 3]

Beyond chess, the product direction is a place for friends to play the games they want, with the same respect for their time. Chess supplies the first working example. Additional games should preserve the reason for gathering and let the session reach a satisfying end. A larger selection is useful when it helps particular people enjoy time together; adding games solely to keep someone browsing would work against the thesis.

The September 10 request to let users ask for new games and have them built automatically adds another dimension. It suggests that the collection could grow from what people want to play together. A group could name a missing game, and the software could acquire that capability through an automated building process. This is a recorded future direction, not a feature available today. How requests become reliable, playable games remains an implementation question. [4]

The broader software thesis is that AI can make this kind of particularity more affordable. The early essay stub calls it “software narrows back down”: if the cost of making and changing software falls, more tools can justify their existence by serving one person or a small group well. The bet is that a need can be worth building for even when it would never support a mass-market product. This is the project's hypothesis about what becomes possible; it does not establish that development, maintenance, or operation become free. [2, 5]

The stub also names a useful direction: “bring your own constraints,” or BYOC. The mechanics of an activity can be shared, while the choices about how it enters someone's life remain particular. Chess needs correct rules and dependable synchronization. A group may also want friends-only opponents and invitations only at agreed times. These are different kinds of decisions, and keeping them separable could let people shape the experience without rebuilding its foundations. BYOC is a design idea at this point; the current app does not offer a general constraint editor. The stub's suggestion of an open-source core is also a proposal, not a change to this repository's licensing. [5]

The same principle could apply to other activities. A writing environment could embody the writer's chosen practice, including when to draft and when to revise. A reading tool could help someone finish a finite selection they chose. These are illustrations of the broader thesis, not additions to the Two Chairs roadmap. The shared question is whether the tool helps a person carry out an intention that would otherwise be lost inside a more general product.

AI does not settle whose intention the software serves. Cheaply generated variety could also produce an endless supply of reasons to keep playing. For Two Chairs, automated creation has to remain accountable to the same product choices as a manually built game. The useful outcome is that friends can ask for something they want to do together and get a dependable way to do it. An automated game factory that undermined their chosen limits would fail that test.

As a working criterion for future decisions, ask whether a change helps people arrange, enjoy, and finish the time they intended to spend together. Returning voluntarily to see a friend can be a success. Leaving after a satisfying game can be a success too. These are proposed ways to judge the product, not a new analytics specification. The questions still open are which games people want next, how much control they want over constraints, and how an automated build earns enough trust to become part of their shared play.
